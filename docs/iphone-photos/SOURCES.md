# almost good: sources

Every claim the post makes about evidence, and where it comes from. Each
source was opened and read (abstract or full text) before it was cited.
Where no evidence was found, the post says so instead of citing anything.

## Composition

- **Palmer, Gardner & Wickens (2008).** *Aesthetic issues in spatial
  composition: effects of position and direction on framing single objects.*
  Spatial Vision 21(3–5). <https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/PalmerGardner%26Wickens-1.pdf>
  - Front-facing subjects: preference peaks at or near the center (the
    "center bias").
  - Side-facing subjects: preferred facing into the frame (the "inward
    bias"); off-center only works when the subject faces inward.
  - Experiment 2 peaks were around 42% / 58% across, with "no evidence
    favoring strong peaks at the 33 and 67 percent positions".
  - Summary line: consistent with the rule of thirds "at best, one-third of
    the time". Caveat: plain displays with no background.
- **Amirshahi, Hayn-Leichsenring, Denzler & Redies (2014).** *Evaluating the
  Rule of Thirds in Photographs and Paintings.* Art & Perception 2.
  <https://doi.org/10.1163/22134913-00002024>
  - Aesthetic ratings correlated only weakly with how much a photo followed
    the rule; highly rated photos and paintings scored about as low on it as
    photos that didn't follow it. "Only a minor, if any, role."
- **Ke, Tang & Jing (2006).** *The Design of High-Level Features for Photo
  Quality Assessment.* CVPR. <https://mmlab.ie.cuhk.edu.hk/pdf/keTJ_CVPR06.pdf>
  - Interviewees named simplicity (obvious subject, easy to separate from
    the background) as the main difference between professional photos and
    snapshots. Snapshots had more background clutter (edges spread to the
    borders); professional photos' edges were concentrated toward the
    center. 72% classification accuracy overall.
- Phi grid, leading lines, headroom: no study found. The post says so.

## Light

- **Johnston, Hill & Carman (1992).** *Recognising faces: effects of lighting
  direction, inversion, and brightness reversal.* Perception 21(3).
  <https://doi.org/10.1068/p210365> (abstract read via Europe PMC, PMID 1437456)
  - The visual system assumes light comes from above; lighting faces from
    below "disrupts the formation of surface-based representations of
    facial shape".
- Hard vs soft light depending on the size of the source: geometry, not a
  study. The post says so.

## Exposure, Night mode, Portrait mode, lenses (Apple)

- iPhone User Guide, *Use iPhone camera tools to set up your shot*:
  <https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/use-camera-tools-iph3dc593597/ios>
  - Camera sets focus and exposure automatically; "face detection balances
    the exposure across many faces"; tap to move the focus area, drag up or
    down beside it to change exposure; touch and hold for AE/AF Lock.
- Apple Support, *Use Night mode on your iPhone*: <https://support.apple.com/en-us/102519>
  - Turns on automatically in low light (icon turns yellow); can take
    several seconds; hold steady or use a surface/tripod; crosshairs when it
    detects movement; turning on Live Photos or flash deactivates Night mode.
- Apple Support, *Use Portrait mode on your iPhone*: <https://support.apple.com/en-us/102398>
  - Portrait mode "creates a depth-of-field effect"; Depth Control adjusts
    background blur afterwards; Portrait Lighting effects.
- iPhone User Guide, *Use Photographic Styles*: <https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/use-photographic-styles-iph629d2cd37/ios>
  - Styles adjust specific colors in different parts of a photo; can be
    edited later in Photos on supported models.
- iPhone User Guide, *iPhone camera basics*: <https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/camera-basics-iph263472f78/ios>
  - 0.5x / 1x / 2x… zoom buttons depending on model.
- Apple, *iPhone 17 technical specifications*: <https://www.apple.com/iphone-17/specs/>
  - Ultra Wide 13 mm, Main 26 mm, 2x Telephoto 52 mm (35 mm equivalents).
    The lens figure uses these to work out how far back each lens puts you.

## Lens distance and faces

- **Ward, Ward, Fried & Paskhover (2018).** *Nasal Distortion in
  Short-Distance Photographs: The Selfie Effect.* JAMA Facial Plastic
  Surgery 20(4). <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5876805/>
  - A geometric model: at 12 inches the nose looks about 30% (men) / 29%
    (women) wider relative to the cheekbones; at 5 feet, essentially no
    difference.
- **Bryan, Perona & Adolphs (2012).** *Perspective Distortion from
  Interpersonal Distance Is an Implicit Visual Cue for Social Judgments of
  Faces.* PLoS ONE 7(9). <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0045301>
  - Faces photographed from within personal space got lower trust-game
    investments and lower ratings of trustworthiness, competence and
    attractiveness than the same faces from further away; controlled for
    image size, expression and lighting.

## Background blur

- Wikipedia, *Depth of field*, "Foreground and background blur":
  <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_of_field#Foreground_and_background_blur>
  - Blur disk b = d · m · x / D (aperture × subject magnification ×
    subject-to-background distance ÷ camera-to-background distance). The
    blur figure draws exactly this, relative, not any phone's real numbers.
